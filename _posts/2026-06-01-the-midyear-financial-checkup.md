Most business owners look closely at their numbers twice a year: when they file taxes and when something goes wrong. Both moments are too late to change the outcome.

June is different. You have six months of real data, which is enough to see patterns, and six months left to act on them. A focused midyear checkup is one of the highest-value hours you can spend on your business. Here are the seven numbers we review with clients every summer, and what each one is really telling you.

## 1. Year-to-date revenue, compared two ways

Start with the obvious one, but compare it properly. Look at first-half revenue against the same period last year, and against what you planned for this year.

Growth against last year tells you about momentum. The gap against your plan tells you whether your expectations, your hiring and your spending are still realistic. If you are running 15 percent behind plan, every decision you made assuming the plan would hold deserves a second look.

## 2. Gross margin

Revenue can grow while the business quietly gets less profitable. Gross margin, the share of each sale left after the direct cost of delivering it, is where that shows up first.

Watch for slow drift. A margin that slips from 42 percent to 38 percent over six months often points to supplier price increases you have not passed on, discounting that crept into the sales process, or labor costs rising faster than prices.

> Revenue tells you how busy you are. Margin tells you whether being busy is worth it.

## 3. Operating cash flow

Profit and cash are not the same thing, and the gap between them catches a lot of owners off guard. Operating cash flow shows how much cash your day-to-day business actually generated, after accounting for customers who have not paid yet, inventory you bought and bills you have not paid.

If your profit and loss statement looks healthy but your bank balance keeps shrinking, this number will usually explain why.

## 4. Days sales outstanding

Days sales outstanding, or DSO, measures how long it takes customers to pay you. If your terms are 30 days and your DSO is 52, you are effectively lending your customers money for three extra weeks.

A rising DSO is an early warning. It often means invoices are going out late, follow-up is inconsistent, or a large customer is struggling. Fixing it can free up a meaningful amount of cash without selling anything new.

## 5. Your largest expense categories

Pull your top five expense categories and compare each one to last year. You are not looking for every penny. You are looking for categories growing faster than revenue.

Software subscriptions, contract labor and marketing spend are common culprits. They creep up one small decision at a time, and nobody notices until they are reviewed together.

## 6. Owner compensation and distributions

How much have you paid yourself so far this year, and in what form? Salary, distributions and reimbursements are treated very differently for tax purposes, and the right mix depends on how your business is structured.

Midyear is the right time to make sure your compensation still fits your entity type and your profit level, rather than finding out in December that you should have adjusted months ago.

## 7. A rough tax projection

You do not need a perfect number. Take your year-to-date profit, project it through December, and estimate what you will owe. Then compare that to the estimated payments you have already made.

If there is a gap, you have time to close it with smarter planning instead of a large check in April. If you are paying more than you need to, you can adjust your remaining estimates and keep that cash working in the business.

## Make it a habit, not an event

The real value of a midyear checkup is not the single review. It is building the habit of looking at the right numbers on a regular rhythm, so nothing important goes unnoticed for long.

That is exactly what good monthly bookkeeping and reporting provide.
