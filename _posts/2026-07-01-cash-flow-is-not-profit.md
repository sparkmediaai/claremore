You had a record month. Your profit and loss statement says so. Then you log in to your bank account and wonder where it all went.

This is one of the most common conversations we have with business owners, and it is not a sign that something is broken. It is a sign that profit and cash flow measure two different things. Understanding the difference is one of the most valuable financial skills an owner can build.

## Profit is a scorecard. Cash is the fuel.

Your profit and loss statement records revenue when you earn it and expenses when you incur them. That is the right way to judge whether your business model works.

Your bank account only cares about when money actually moves. A sale you made in June might not turn into cash until August. A piece of equipment you bought in May drains your account all at once, even though it only shows up on your P&L a little at a time.

> A business does not fail because it is unprofitable on paper. It fails because it runs out of cash before the profit arrives.

## The five usual suspects

When profit and cash drift apart, it is almost always one of these.

### 1. Customers who have not paid yet

Every invoice that is out but unpaid is profit you have earned but cannot spend. If your sales are growing, this gap usually grows with them, which is why fast growth can feel like a cash crunch.

### 2. Inventory and supplies

Buying inventory turns cash into product sitting on a shelf. It does not hit your profit until it sells. Overstocking is one of the quietest ways to tie up cash.

### 3. Loan principal payments

Interest shows up as an expense. The principal portion of your loan payments does not appear on your P&L at all, but it leaves your bank account every month.

### 4. Large purchases

Equipment, vehicles and buildouts are spread across several years on your financial statements, but you often pay for them up front.

### 5. Owner draws and distributions

Money you take out of the business is not an expense on most P&L statements, so it is easy to underestimate how much it affects your cash position.

## Build a 13-week cash forecast

If you adopt one tool from this article, make it this one. A 13-week forecast is a simple, week-by-week view of the next quarter:

- Your starting cash balance
- Cash you expect to receive each week, based on real invoices and realistic payment timing
- Cash you expect to pay out each week, including payroll, rent, loan payments, taxes and planned purchases
- Your projected ending balance for each week

Thirteen weeks is long enough to see a problem coming and short enough to forecast with reasonable accuracy. Update it every week, and it will tell you weeks in advance when you need to slow spending, push collections or line up financing.

## Five ways to improve cash flow without selling more

1. **Invoice immediately.** Every day between finishing the work and sending the invoice is a day added to your wait.
2. **Make paying easy.** Accept cards and electronic payments, and include a payment link on every invoice.
3. **Tighten terms where you can.** Moving new customers from 45 days to 30 days, or requiring deposits on large projects, can make a real difference.
4. **Follow up on a schedule.** A polite, automated reminder a few days before and after the due date collects more than an occasional phone call.
5. **Match big purchases with financing.** Paying cash for long-lived equipment can starve the business of working capital. Sometimes financing is the smarter choice.
